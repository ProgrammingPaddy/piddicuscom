How roman numerals work

A numeral names a chord by the scale degree it is built on, not by its letter. In C major the chord on degree 5 is G, so G is written V. In E major, V is B. The letter changes with the key; the relationship does not. That is the whole point: numerals describe the job a chord does in its key, so a progression written in numerals works in every key. Tell a band "one, five, six, four" and it works whether the singer wants G or E♭.

Reading the symbols

  • UPPERCASE means major: I, IV, V.
  • lowercase means minor: ii, iii, vi.
  • ° means diminished (vii°); ø means half-diminished, an m7♭5 chord (viiø7); + means augmented (III+).
  • A trailing 7, 9, or 13 means those extensions are stacked on top: V7, ii9. maj7 marks the major seventh specifically: Imaj7.
  • sus written out means the 3rd is replaced (Vsus4), so the chord is neither major nor minor. Power chords take the plain numeral by rock convention; with no 3rd, the letter case cannot mean anything anyway.

How professionals actually use these: analysis keeps numerals to standard chord types. Added color tones do not change the numeral (a Cadd4 chord in C major is still just I; the 4 is treated as texture, not harmony), and sonorities without a conventional label, like quartal stacks or a seventh chord missing its 3rd, simply do not get a numeral. This tool follows that practice: if no honest standard numeral exists, none is shown.

Why numerals change when the scale changes

Each chord in the palette is built by stacking every other note of the current scale. Change the scale and the ingredient notes change, so the chords built from them change too. Natural minor stacks a weak minor chord on degree 5 (v). Harmonic minor exists largely to fix that: raising the 7th degree turns v into a real V7 that pulls hard back to home. Switch between Minor and Harm. Minor and watch degree 5 change.

Functions: T, PD, D

In major and minor keys, chords group into three jobs. Tonic (T) is home: I and vi. Pre-dominant (PD) moves away from home: ii and IV. Dominant (D) builds the tension that resolves home: V and vii°. Most progressions are loops through home, away, tension, home.

Numerals in the wild

  • Pop: the four-chord loop I V vi IV (or rotated, vi IV I V) powers an enormous share of the charts. Same numerals, thousands of songs.
  • 50s and doo-wop: I vi IV V, the "Stand By Me" changes.
  • Rock: loves borrowing from Mixolydian: I ♭VII IV is the classic-rock move, and minor-key rock leans on i ♭VI ♭VII.
  • Blues: the 12-bar form is I7 IV7 V7 with dominant 7ths on every chord, cheerfully breaking diatonic rules. That rule-breaking is the blues sound.
  • Jazz: the basic cell is ii7 V7 Imaj7, chained through different keys.
  • Flamenco and surf: the Andalusian cadence i ♭VII ♭VI V.

A good exercise: pick a key, then play any of these patterns from the palette in order and listen for the pull back to I.

The 12-note system

Western music runs on 12 notes: C, C♯, D, E♭, E, F, F♯, G, A♭, A, B♭, B, then C again. That second C is an octave up, and its frequency is exactly double. Every octave repeats the same 12 names, higher or lower; that is why the piano keyboard is the same pattern over and over.

Equal temperament

12-tone equal temperament (12-TET) divides the octave into 12 identical steps called semitones. Each semitone multiplies the frequency by the 12th root of 2, about 1.0595, so twelve steps land exactly on the doubled frequency. A4 is 440 Hz, so A5 is 880.

The payoff of making every step identical: every key sounds the same, just higher or lower. A major scale starting on C uses the same step recipe as one starting on F♯, and the recipe is all that matters. That is why the key pills work at all; change the key and every relationship survives intact.

What it looks like on the instruments

On the piano, one semitone is the very next key, black or white. On the guitar, one fret is one semitone, and the frets crowd together up the neck because each step is a frequency ratio rather than a fixed distance; the fret spacing drawn here is the real geometry.

Sharps and flats

In 12-TET, C♯ and D♭ are the same pitch. Which spelling is correct depends on musical context (the same key on the piano is spelled C♯ in D major but D♭ in A♭ major); this tool uses one fixed spelling per note to keep the interface simple.

Scales, keys, chords

A scale is a subset of the 12 notes, usually 7 of them, chosen by a step recipe. A key is a scale plus a home note. Chords are built by stacking scale members. Everything on this page is arithmetic on the same 12 notes.

Why 12 in the first place? Dividing the octave into 12 lands remarkably close to the pure ratios ears gravitate toward: 7 semitones is within a tenth of a percent of a perfect 3:2 fifth. Musicians tuned to pure ratios for centuries; 12-TET trades a sliver of that purity for the freedom to play equally well in every key.

How chord names work

A chord symbol is a recipe read left to right: root letter, then quality, then extras. A bare letter like C means a C major triad (C E G); everything after the letter modifies that default.

Quality

  • C: major triad, 1 3 5. Major is the unmarked default.
  • Cm: minor. The 3rd is lowered a semitone (1 ♭3 5) and nothing else changes.
  • Cdim (1 ♭3 ♭5) and Caug (1 3 ♯5): the triad squeezed smaller or stretched larger.
  • Csus2 / Csus4: the 3rd is replaced by the 2nd or 4th. With no 3rd, the chord is neither major nor minor.
  • C5: root and 5th only, the power chord. Also no 3rd.

Sevenths and extensions

  • C7: major triad plus the lowered 7th (♭7). A plain "7" always means the ♭7; this is the dominant sound.
  • Cmaj7: the raised (major) 7th instead. One semitone, completely different mood.
  • Cm7: minor triad plus ♭7. Cm(maj7): minor triad with the major 7th.
  • C9, C11, C13: keep stacking every other note above the 7th. A 13 chord implies the 7 and 9 underneath it; players drop what they cannot reach.

Add, alter, omit

  • add means the tone joins WITHOUT a 7th: Cadd9 = C E G D. Compare C9, which includes the ♭7.
  • ♭ or ♯ before a number alters that tone: C7♭9, C7♯11. These are the "altered dominant" colors.
  • (no3) means the chord omits its 3rd, the tone that decides major versus minor. C7(no3) is C G B♭: a dominant 7th skeleton with no gender. Rock plays this constantly (a power chord with a ♭7 on top), and big voicings drop the 3rd for space. It is spelled out here so a chord never claims a major or minor sound it does not actually contain.
  • ø7 is half-diminished (m7♭5); °7 is fully diminished. Compact symbols for two dark 7th chords.

Same notes, several names

C6 (C E G A) and Am7 (A C E G) are the same four notes. Which name is right depends on the bass note and the key around it. The interpretation pills exist because that ambiguity is real, not a bug.

How this tool works

Everything responds to everything: pick a chord and both instruments light up; click notes on the piano or guitar and the app names what you played; change the key or scale and the palette, overlays, and explanations follow.

Key & scale strip (top)

  • Key pills set the tonal center; scale pills set the scale, ordered roughly by how common they are. Pin key to root is a toggle: while on, the key follows whatever chord root you select.
  • Scale chips (right) show the scale tones with degree numbers: green background = currently in your chord, gold = tonic, violet = the scale's characteristic tone. Click a chip to hear it.
  • Overlay toggles scale dots on the piano and neck: green dot = in scale, gold dot = the key center.

Chord builder (left)

  • Root / Quality / Extensions build the chord. Blue = selected; green outline = consistent with the notes you clicked by hand.
  • The readout shows the chord name, its roman numeral in the current key, and every reasonable interpretation of the selected notes; click one to adopt it.

Where the explanations live

  • Chord panel (beside the readout): interval-by-interval construction and how the chord relates to the current key.
  • Scale panel (right side of the key strip): the current scale's character, step pattern in semitones, characteristic tone, parent major, and a clickable modes-of-the-major-scale bar from Lydian down to Locrian. The 12-note system button there explains equal temperament, octaves, and why frets crowd together.
  • Voicing info (toggle in the guitar bar): opens an analysis panel beside the neck showing string roles, omissions, and barres for the current shape.

"In this key" palette

The chords that live in the selected key, as Triads / 7ths / 9ths (9ths fall back to the 7th where the diatonic 9th would be a ♭9). The numeral is the scale degree the chord is built on; the How numerals work button next to the palette is the full guide, symbols and real-world progression examples included. Hovering any chord shows exactly which scale tones were stacked to build it. Corner letters: T tonic, PD pre-dominant, D dominant (major/minor keys). A violet dot marks chords containing the scale's characteristic tone; lean on those to sound like the mode. Pentatonic/blues scales show their degree stacks as triads.

Guitar neck

  • The voicing engine searches real string/fret combinations under hand constraints and ranks playable shapes; classic shapes rank first when they exist. Browse with ◀ ▶.
  • Muted strings show a red × and a red-tinted string. ○ = open string. Gold dot = root. The engine drops the most expendable tones (5th first) when a chord has more notes than strings.
  • Labels: Chord shows just the voicing (plus small scale dots on the string when Overlay is on); Scale names every in-key note on the whole neck; All notes names everything, with out-of-key notes dimmed.
  • Notes / Fingers switches chord-dot labels between note names and a suggested fingering (barres shown as finger 1; it's a heuristic, trust your hands).
  • Tuning and capo recompute everything; fret numbers with a capo are relative to the capo, like real chord charts. The neck shows 18 frets by default and can switch to 24. Voicings respect the selected inversion: the lowest sounding string carries the bass.
  • Clicking frets builds your own shape: one note per string, exactly where you click. The app names what you built but never adds notes to it. Click a placed note to remove it; clicking a fret on an occupied string moves that string's note. Choosing a chord (builder, palette, or a pill) switches back to engine voicings and replaces your shape.
  • Each string has three states: untouched (nothing shown), open, or muted. The column at the nut cycles them: click once for an open string (○), again to mute it (red ×), again to clear. Fretted, open, and muted are all part of the shape; untouched strings are simply undecided.

Playback & mixing

  • Play as chooses which instrument sounds when you select chords or hit Play: piano plays the selected notes, guitar strums the current voicing.
  • Multi-press stacks clicked notes; single-note mode replaces. Arpeggio vs block, sustain, Oct −/+ as labeled. Strum plays the guitar voicing low-to-high.
  • Keys / Guitar / Master sliders mix the two instruments.
  • Computer keys A W S E D F T G Y H U J K play a chromatic octave from C.
